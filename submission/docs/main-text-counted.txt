1. Project definition and scope

Incomplete bug reports create clarification work before debugging. Bettenburg et al. (2008) found mismatches between developers' information needs and reporters' submissions. Our helper prompts student teams for reproducible descriptions; reduced clarification is the intended value, to be evaluated rather than assumed. Topic approval was confirmed by the repository owner; no approval record is attached.

Reporters need understandable prompts, developers need reproduction details, testers need expected/actual outcomes, team leads need status visibility, and the lecturer needs traceable assessment evidence. Scope is seven fields (title, description, steps, expected behaviour, actual behaviour, environment, severity), local persistence, listing/status changes, completeness guidance and filtering. Authentication, public hosting, AI diagnosis, attachments and external tracker integration are excluded.

Four members, two short iterations and limited availability constrain delivery. Three engineering issues drive our choices: evolving guidance requirements, conflicting service/schema changes, and misleading or incomplete reports. The product is a single-instance local prototype; the shared repository coordinates development. No existing application code was imported; Python/SQLite and acknowledged research are reused.

2. Comparison and justified method selection

We compare plan-driven development with iterative-incremental development using agile practices against the same criteria (Table 1). Iteration describes repeated development and delivery; agile adds feedback and adaptation, so the labels are not mutually exclusive (Larman & Basili, 2003).

Table 1. Alternatives under the project constraints

Decision factor

Plan-driven approach

Iterative-incremental with agile practices

Requirement change

Baseline fields and approve changes: predictable scope, but early guidance assumptions may cause rework.

Demonstrate reporting before guidance; reprioritise feedback between increments. Changes still consume fixed capacity.

Coordination

Documented responsibilities and interfaces support handovers; sequential work can delay integration feedback.

Shared backlog, short branches and frequent integration expose conflicts early; require reviewer availability.

Quality assurance

Up-front verification and traceability suit stable, consequential requirements; late user validation can miss usability problems.

Acceptance checks and reviews accompany each increment; repeated regression costs time and does not guarantee useful feedback.

Delivery effort

Detailed baselines are useful for storage contracts; comprehensive documentation competes with the small implementation budget.

Small working slices permit early learning; full framework ceremonies would be disproportionate for four people.

We select two timeboxed increments, retaining a field contract and testable acceptance baseline while adapting guidance after demonstration. Boehm and Turner's (2003) risk framework supports this balance: small size, low criticality and uncertain usability favour feedback, while shared persistence needs deliberate design. Scope changes enter the backlog; only urgent defects interrupt an increment. We use prioritisation, reviews and retrospectives without claiming full Scrum adoption.

Research supports a conditional choice. Dybå and Dingsøyr's (2008) systematic review found limited empirical evidence, dominated by early XP studies; it cannot establish universal agile superiority. McIntosh et al. (2014) associate review participation with quality in large open-source systems, supporting substantive review rather than approval counts; causality and transfer to four students remain uncertain. Elazhary et al.'s (2022) three-company study shows CI implementation varies and can constrain feedback. Consequently, our pipeline checks acceptance and compilation on two Python versions; human review examines intent and omissions.

A distributed team would retain increments but add asynchronous decision records, explicit handovers and agreed review windows. Camara et al.'s (2024) distributed large-scale review supports tailoring, although its scale limits direct transfer. A regulated system would require stronger hazard analysis, requirement-test traceability and documented approvals; feedback cycles could remain, with more plan-driven assurance (Boehm & Turner, 2003).
